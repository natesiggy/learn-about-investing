GETTING STARTED

Getting Started in High School

Learn how to choose an account, compare investments, and build a portfolio with the money you have.

Deciding how much to invest

Before choosing investments, separate money you expect to spend soon from money you can leave invested for several years. If college expenses are coming up, keep that portion accessible rather than depending on stock prices being up when tuition is due.

Being young does not automatically mean you should put most of your money into stocks. Your timeline, how much of a loss you could handle, and how comfortable you are with price changes all matter. You can invest part of your money while keeping the rest available for college and everyday expenses.

Opening an account

You buy stocks and funds through a brokerage account. If you are under 18, a parent or guardian will usually need to help you open an eligible teen or custodial account. Compare the account's fees, minimum balance, available investments, and rules about who controls the money. Check whether it offers fractional shares and recurring purchases.

Once the account is set up, you can transfer money and choose an investment. Transferring cash into the account does not automatically invest it. You still need to select what to buy.

Starting with a broad index fund

A low-cost fund that follows a broad stock-market index is a useful option to research for money you can leave invested. Instead of depending on one company, it spreads your investment across many businesses. One broad fund can be a simpler starting point than trying to pick several winning stocks.

Look at what the fund actually owns and its expense ratio. A fund focused on one industry can still be concentrated, even if it holds several companies. Owning a few funds with nearly identical holdings does not add much variety.

Using fractional shares

Some brokerages let you buy a dollar amount of a stock or ETF instead of a whole share. If one share costs $200, you could invest $50 and own a quarter of a share. This lets you choose an amount that fits your plan rather than choosing an investment just because its share price is low. Availability depends on the brokerage and investment.

Adding money regularly

You could choose a manageable amount to invest each month or whenever you get paid. Investing the same dollar amount at regular intervals is called dollar-cost averaging. You buy more shares when the price is lower and fewer when it is higher, without having to guess the best day to buy.

If you already have money set aside to invest, you can invest it at once or spread purchases over a few dates. Spreading purchases can make a price drop right after the first purchase easier to handle. Investing sooner gives more of your money time in the market. Neither approach removes market risk, and spreading purchases does not always produce a better result.

Keeping costs low

Compare fund expenses, account fees, and any trading charges. A $5 monthly fee adds up to $60 each year before considering any missed growth. That matters more when your balance is smaller. A brokerage advertising commission-free trades may still have other costs.

Buying and selling frequently can also increase costs and create taxable gains. A straightforward approach is to buy investments you understand, add money when you can, and review your choices occasionally instead of reacting to every price change.

Learning about individual stocks

If researching companies interests you, you can use a small portion of your investing money for individual stocks while keeping most of your stock exposure diversified. Read about how the business makes money, its competition, and its debt. Decide how much you could lose before buying, rather than choosing an amount because a stock is popular.

A practice portfolio is another way to compare your stock choices with a broad fund before putting more money into them. Keep notes on why you chose each investment and what you learned.

Why learning now helps

Starting in high school gives you experience comparing fees, understanding price changes, and making decisions before you have larger amounts to manage. Returns left invested can earn further returns over time. Regular contributions add to that growth, so your progress does not depend entirely on picking a stock that rises quickly.
