Choosing a mix in high school

Start with when you will need the money. Keep upcoming college costs and money for everyday expenses separate from the part you can leave invested. The mixes below are starting points to consider for that investing portion, not percentages you need to apply to all your money.

Index funds are included in the stock or bond allocation, depending on what they hold. A stock index fund counts as stocks, so you would not add it as a fourth category. For a beginner, a broad stock index fund can make up most or all of the stock portion.

Lower risk

This mix may suit a student who wants some experience investing but would find a large loss hard to handle. Most of the money stays outside stocks. For the bond portion, research short-term, high-quality bonds or funds rather than assuming every bond is low risk. Bonds can still fall in value. Money you definitely need for tuition soon may belong entirely in insured savings or a suitable CD instead of this mix.

Moderate risk

This mix may suit a student who can leave the investing portion alone for several years and is comfortable seeing its value fall at times. A broad stock index fund provides the main growth exposure, while bonds and cash reduce the amount depending on stock prices. The 10% cash here is separate from money already set aside for college.

Higher risk

This mix may suit a student whose college and near-term expenses are covered separately, who can leave the money invested for many years, and who can handle a larger drop without needing to sell. Higher risk does not have to mean buying speculative stocks. Most or all of the 80% stock portion could still be in broad index funds. If you want to try individual stocks, count them within that stock percentage and keep them a small part of it.
